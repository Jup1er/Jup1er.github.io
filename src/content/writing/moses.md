The sunlight travels at its own pace—piercing through the air, through the glass, through the crowds—to arrive at the sculpture that is caressed by human gazes every single day. Those photons do not linger, yet they leave behind a subtle warmth, allowing the marble's breath to shift continuously over the centuries.

The photons reflect onto my cheek and sink deep into my skin. A single crystal tear streams down my left eye, rolling toward the place where the light penetrates. And there, I see it. It is the faith of the soul; the lonely pride of the awakened; the passing of thought. It is the stubbornness of gazing dimly toward the truth; the limitation of being unable to clear the fog within one's mind; the sheer madness of seeking the universe through that very mist.

It is also... being misunderstood and betrayed by one's own faith.

If faith collapses, then what is this truth I have been pursuing?  
It had never occurred to me that faith and truth were not one and the same.  
Could it be that my faith has become the counterforce, pushing me toward the truth?  
Why has my faith become the very shape of what you once criticized?  
That so-called faith defined truth, yet betrayed it.

Then what am I still drawing closer to?  
I am drawing closer to myself.

As it turns out, the so-called God is all things, not any separate, solitary entity.  
It must be a grander, formless love—one that nevertheless envelops all fragility and struggle.

The tear evaporates quietly when unobserved. I look back at the lines of the statue—from his hands, to his legs, to the strained muscles on his arms. Then, I suddenly look up. As I observe his features and his expression, the marble suddenly quickens with the life of Moses.

I feel Moses’ fury, his powerlessness, his bewilderment: *They speak of tolerance, yet exclude dissent; they speak of loving mankind, yet punish doubt.* I also feel the murmurs of understanding, coming from countless others who have read him just as deeply. And then there is Michelangelo, the equally lonely artist who gave life to this stone. With his own hands, he carved the image of God, yet embedded a defiance against God within every single chisel mark.

Or perhaps, God never demanded unconditional obedience; He never intended to give humanity a comfortable, thoughtless faith. Instead, through trials, conflicts, and contradictions, He forces us to think, to question, and to choose.

Whose rules are these anyway? *I have told you of good and evil; now is the time for you to judge for yourselves.* For good and evil are not things to be told; they are rebuilt only after being experienced, questioned, and even completely overthrown.

Perhaps true awakening is learning to become the creator of one's own faith, right there in the mire of humanity.

Does the answer, after all, only exist on the other side of doubt and relentless questioning?
